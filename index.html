Within-county variation

Only 349 of 9,129 tracts meet CV ≤ 30% and a denominator of at least 50 women. This filter favors larger uninsured estimates: the reliable subset does not represent all California neighborhoods. Read patterns across groups of tracts, not rankings of individual tracts.

What the average can hide

Reliable tract rates will appear for the selected county. Some counties have no tracts meeting this rule.

Concentration

Tracts ranked from higher to lower uninsured rate. Includes low-reliability estimates; excludes denominators below 50. This is a descriptive curve, not a stable priority ranking.

The top 899 of 8,985 non-suppressed tracts (top 10%, rounded up) contain 28.95% of statewide estimated uninsured women. Ties use GEOID order. The excluded small-denominator tracts contain 96 uninsured women, so the curve ends just below 100%.

Between counties, within counties

County membership explains 8.35% of population-weighted variation in rates across 8,985 non-suppressed tracts; 91.65% is within counties. Among 349 reliable tracts, those shares are 23.08% between and 76.92% within counties.

Population weights use women ages 19–44. Observed variation includes sampling noise and should not be interpreted as true neighborhood inequality.

Counts are survey-estimated women, not patients. Insurance is an access proxy, not care received.

What goes with higher rates?

Poverty describes all people for whom poverty status is determined. Medicaid describes the civilian noninstitutionalized population of all ages and both sexes. These covariate universes differ from women ages 19–44.

Poverty & uninsured rate

Medicaid & uninsured rate

Reliable-only, unweighted OLS (n = 349, R² = 0.064): a 1 percentage-point increase in poverty is associated with 0.172 percentage points in uninsured rate, and a 1-point increase in Medicaid share with 0.006 points, adjusting for the other covariate and rurality. Relative to urban, coefficients are -4.394 points for large rural, -0.828 for small rural, and -5.702 for isolated; intercept 20.113. These are exploratory associations without causal or statistical-significance claims. Reliable group sizes: large rural 5, small rural 1, isolated 11, urban 332.

Associations describe places, not individuals, and do not establish causation. The reliability filter selects a small, atypical subset. Rural groups are particularly small; regression does not account for spatial dependence, survey-design covariance, or measurement error in the covariates.

The measure

U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2020–2024 five-year estimates, table B27001. Uninsured women in the 19–25, 26–34 and 35–44 age groups are summed and divided by the total female population in those same groups. The universe is the civilian noninstitutionalized population. The source records sex; it does not identify PCOS status or gender identity.

Tract data checks

9,129 ACS tracts: 90 with zero women and 54 with 1–49 women; 144 suppressed, 8,636 low reliability, and 349 reliable. 8,985 have denominators of at least 50 (usable for descriptive concentration, not necessarily reliable). Reliable rate distribution: min 4.38%, Q1 18.25%, median 22.61%, Q3 27.65%, max 45.97%; moment skewness 0.359. Geometry: 9,109; ACS: 9,129; matched: 9,109; geometry-only: 0; ACS-only: 20 zero-population tracts omitted by the cartographic geometry. RUCA matches all 9,129 ACS tracts. Tract sums exactly match all 58 county numerator and denominator counts: 571,146 / 6,939,418 = 8.2305% statewide, rounding to 8.2%. The largest rate difference from the rounded county CSV is 0.000500 percentage points.

Tract methods

ACS 2020–2024 B27001 female total cells 037, 040, 043 and uninsured cells 039, 042, 045. Count MOEs use the square root of summed squared MOEs. Rate MOEs use the Census proportion formula, falling back to the ratio formula when its radicand is negative; approximate 90% display intervals are clipped to 0–100%. CV = (rate MOE / 1.645) / rate. Reliable means CV ≤ 30%; low means CV > 30% or undefined (including zero uninsured estimates); denominators below 50 are suppressed on the map and in displayed rates. Survey uncertainty remains even in the reliable subset.

Poverty is B17001_002E / B17001_001E. Medicaid/means-tested coverage is C27007 cells 004, 007, 010, 014, 017, 020 divided by 001 (all ages and both sexes, not women 19–44). Median household income is B19013_001E, in 2024 dollars. Missing/sentinel values remain missing.

Tract polygons use Census 2020 cartographic boundaries at 1:500,000, simplified with shared boundaries preserved. These are display geometry, not precise neighborhood limits. USDA 2020 RUCA joins directly on its TractFIPS20 field: no guessed 2010 crosswalk. Primary codes 1–3 are urban, 4–6 large rural, 7–9 small rural, and 10 isolated; code 99 is unknown. RUCA uses the 2020 Census and 2017–2021 commuting flows, a different period from the insurance estimates.

The county geography

California's 58 counties, from the official California GIS service credited to the Department of Finance. Boundaries and estimates connect through five-character county FIPS identifiers. County scale provides complete coverage, but conceals neighborhood differences. The boundary service does not establish an exact 2024 vintage. Its older population attributes are not used.

The interpretation

PCOS data are not available at this scale, so PCOS is context only. Tract estimates have wide margins of error and are best read as patterns across groups of tracts, not rankings of individual tracts. ACS five-year estimates are not a current snapshot.

Insurance coverage is one dimension of potential access. These data do not measure specialist availability, affordability, insurance acceptance, actual care received, or unmet need among PCOS patients. They cannot establish causation. Small-county estimates can be unstable, and overlapping intervals should discourage precise rankings. Intervals displayed in charts are clipped to 0–100%.
